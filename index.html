A quick history of Copenhagen and Denmark

Copenhagen is a city of islands and harbors that grew from a Viking-age fishing settlement into the capital of one of the oldest kingdoms in the world. These are the key moments that shaped what you will see today. Each stop on the route links back to one of them.

  • c. 960sDenmark takes shape. King Gorm the Old and his son Harald Bluetooth unite the country and, around 965, Harald converts to Christianity. The Jelling stones, the ‘birth certificate of Denmark’, are the first place the name appears. Denmark is one of the oldest monarchies in the world.
  • 790s to 1035The Viking Age. Danish raiders and settlers reach England, where the Danelaw takes root. In 1016 Cnut the Great becomes King of England and later rules England, Denmark and Norway as a ‘North Sea Empire’.
  • 1167Copenhagen is born. A fishing and trading harbor called Havn (‘harbor’) already stood here. Bishop Absalon, a warrior and churchman, builds a fortress on the islet of Slotsholmen to defend it against Wendish pirates.
  • 1254The harbor town receives its city charter and grows into a trading hub, later called København, ‘merchants’ harbor’.
  • 1368 to 1369The Hanseatic League, the powerful German merchant alliance, attacks Copenhagen and destroys Absalon’s castle.
  • 1397 to 1523The Kalmar Union joins Denmark, Norway and Sweden under one crown. Around 1417 Copenhagen becomes the royal capital, and in 1479 the University of Copenhagen is founded. Sweden leaves the union in 1523.
  • 1536The Reformation. Denmark becomes Lutheran and the crown takes over church property.
  • 1588 to 1648The builder-king Christian IV. He gives the city Rosenborg, Børsen, the Round Tower and Nyboder, and expands the navy.
  • 1658 to 1660The wars with Sweden. In 1658 the Swedish army crosses the frozen Great Belt and Denmark loses large territories in the Treaty of Roskilde. In 1659 Copenhagen survives a Swedish siege. In 1660 the monarchy becomes absolute.
  • 1700sDisasters and rebuilding. The plague of 1711 kills about a third of the city, the great fires of 1728 and 1795 destroy huge parts of the old town, and Christiansborg burns in 1794. Amalienborg becomes the royal residence.
  • 1801 to 1814The Napoleonic Wars. Nelson attacks the Danish fleet in 1801. In 1807 the British bombard Copenhagen and take the fleet. The state goes bankrupt in 1813, and in 1814 Denmark loses Norway to Sweden.
  • 1800 to 1850The Golden Age. In a poorer, smaller Denmark, Hans Christian Andersen, the philosopher Søren Kierkegaard and the sculptor Thorvaldsen make Copenhagen a capital of culture. The city is rebuilt in a neoclassical style.
  • 1849The absolute monarchy ends. A new constitution on 5 June creates a parliament. The old ramparts are torn down from the 1850s and the city expands.
  • 1864The Second Schleswig War. Denmark loses Schleswig-Holstein to Prussia and Austria and turns inward, with a new focus on neutrality.
  • 1940 to 1945Occupation. Germany invades on 9 April 1940 and Denmark surrenders within hours. In October 1943 most of the country’s Jews are smuggled to Sweden. Liberation comes on 5 May 1945.
  • 1949 to todayA modern Denmark. It joins NATO in 1949 and the European Community in 1973. The Øresund Bridge to Sweden opens in 2000, and the driverless Copenhagen Metro opens in 2002. In 2004 Crown Prince Frederik marries Mary Donaldson in the Cathedral, and in 2024 he becomes King Frederik X after Queen Margrethe II abdicates.

The route

Hygge for two

Hygge (say “HOO-ga”) is the Danish art of being cozy: candles, warm drinks, soft blankets and good company. In November it is practically the national sport.

  • Try: smørrebrød (open-faced rye sandwiches), wienerbrød (Danish pastries), æbleskiver (little puffy pancake balls, a Christmas classic) and gløgg (spiced mulled wine).
  • Lunch: Nyhavn has lots of terraces with blankets and heaters. The times already include a 45-minute lunch break at Nyhavn. If you are hungry earlier, Torvehallerne, where you start, is perfect for a first coffee and pastry.
  • Pocket Danish: Hej (hi), Tak (thank you), Skål (cheers), Kys (kiss) and Jeg elsker dig (I love you).

Practical tips

  • Concert night: leave the hotel around 17:50 for the Royal Arena at 18:30. Charge your phones, have your tickets ready and check the arena’s bag rules.
  • Weather: expect roughly 0 to 7 °C. Wear layers, a hat and gloves.
  • Metro (M3): if you get tired, use the stations on the route: Nørreport, Østerport, Marmorkirken, Kongens Nytorv, Gammel Strand and Rådhuspladsen.
  • Paying: Denmark uses the krone (DKK) and cards work almost everywhere.
  • Shoes: lots of cobblestones. Wear comfy ones.
  • Golden hour: the light is softest over the harbor in the afternoon, so save the big photos for Nyhavn and the Little Mermaid.
